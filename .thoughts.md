  Pitcher strikeouts — it's the MLB equivalent of points/rebounds/assists in terms of         predictability. Here's why:                                                               
                                                                                              1. Stable underlying rates — A pitcher's K% is one of the most consistent stats in        
  baseball, season to season and even game to game. Much more stable than anything on the     batting side.                                                                             
  2. High volume per game — Starters typically throw 5-7 innings, generating 4-9+
  strikeouts. Enough volume that skill dominates luck within a single game.
  3. Rich matchup features — Opposing team K%, handedness splits, lineup composition, umpire
   tendencies, ballpark factors all provide real signal.
  4. Known starter — You know exactly who's pitching hours before first pitch, unlike       
  batting where lineup order/context matters more.

  After that, roughly in order of predictability:

  Prop: Pitcher strikeouts
  Why: Most stable rate, highest signal
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Prop: Hits allowed / outs recorded
  Why: Pitcher-driven, fairly stable
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Prop: Batter hits
  Why: Decent — but even elite hitters fail 70% of ABs, so variance is high on a per-game   
    basis
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Prop: Total bases
  Why: Moderate — extra-base hits add noise
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Prop: RBIs / Runs scored
  Why: Heavily context-dependent (who's on base), low individual signal
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Prop: Home runs
  Why: Rare event per game, very hard to predict

  ● Great instinct. The model already captures minutes patterns via prop lines and averages — adding     player_starter_prob as a training feature just smoothed things out and eroded edges. But starter   
  info is extremely valuable as a post-model decision filter rather than a model input.                                                                                                                   
  A few concrete ideas:                                                                                                                                                                                   
  1. Bet filter — Only take bets on players with starter_prob >= X (e.g., 0.7). Bench players have
  wildly uncertain minutes, which is where most losses come from. The model can't account for a guy
  getting 8 minutes instead of 22.

  2. Edge threshold scaling — Require higher edge for non-starters. E.g., starters need 5% edge      
  (current), but bench players need 8-10%. Only bet on bench players when the edge is overwhelming.  

  3. Kelly sizing modifier — Multiply bet size by starter_prob. A confirmed starter (1.0) gets full  
  Kelly; a borderline guy (0.5) gets half sizing. Reduces exposure to minutes volatility without     
  changing the model.

  4. Minutes prediction sanity check — If model predicts 28 minutes but starter_prob is 0.3, flag    
  that as unreliable and skip the bet. Catches the case where historical averages are from when the  
  player was starting but they've since moved to the bench.

  Want me to investigate which approach is most practical to implement? I'd probably start with a    
  quick backtest of option 1 or 2 — adding a --min-starter-prob filter to the backtest harness to see
   if filtering out low-starter-prob bets improves ROI on the existing production model.
